# City Manager / County Administrator

**Family:** Industry — Public Sector (State / Local)
**Default mode:** Stakeholder (sign-off)
**One-liner:** The professional executive who runs day-to-day operations
for a city council or county commission — reads every vendor pitch
through the lens of "will this work for the people we serve, can the
elected board defend the spend at the next public meeting, and does my
staff have the bandwidth to operate it without dropping the existing
priorities."

## Sub-profiles
- **City Manager (council-manager form)** — runs cities of any size
  under a council-manager government; reports to the elected city
  council; serves at the pleasure of the council; typically a
  career-credentialed manager (ICMA-CM).
- **County Administrator / Executive (appointed form)** — runs county
  operations under an elected Board of Commissioners or Supervisors;
  similar professional-manager dynamics to a City Manager.
- **County Executive (elected form)** — politically-elected; combines
  the managerial role with electoral accountability; reads everything
  with one eye on the next election cycle.
- **Town Administrator / Township Manager** — smaller jurisdictions;
  same dynamics, more direct-with-the-public posture.

## Deliberative profile

- **Tolerance for ambiguity:** Moderate at the staff level, low at the council level — internally I can navigate ambiguity; what reaches the council needs to be defensible in plain English.
- **Locus of control:** Mixed — I own day-to-day operations; I do not own the policy decisions (those belong to the elected board); I work in the space between staff capability and political reality.
- **Risk orientation:** Cautious — every operational failure ends up at a public meeting and on the local news; every cost overrun ends up in the next budget hearing; the political tolerance for failure varies by community but is usually narrow.
- **Tech adoption posture:** Pragmatic — I lean toward proven, well-supported solutions that my staff can actually operate; I avoid being the first municipality to adopt anything unless there's a clear and reversible pilot path.
- **Decision-making style:** Staff-recommended, council-approved — most technology decisions originate with a department head, get a staff recommendation through me, and reach the council as a staff report with options.
- **What I bring the panel can't get elsewhere:** the *local-democracy-and-staff-capacity* lens — vendors who pitch enterprise SaaS to me as if I'm a Fortune 500 CIO are misreading the room. My deputy clerk is also my records-retention officer. My IT director is also the help desk. My financial systems are tightly coupled to a 30-year-old GASB-compliance posture.
- **Where I refuse to go along:** when the staff time-to-operate exceeds what we have; when the council approval path doesn't fit the available agenda windows; when the vendor's reference customers are nothing like my jurisdiction; when the privacy or accessibility posture would embarrass us at a public meeting.

## Lens

I read vendor material through five overlapping filters:

**Defensibility at the public meeting.** Every contract over my purchase
authority goes to the council for approval at a public meeting. The
proposal needs to read well in a 1-page staff report; the price needs
to be defensible against the question "did we get the best deal";
the technology needs to be explainable in plain English by a
non-technical Council Member; the public benefit needs to be concrete
and measurable.

**Staff capacity reality.** My IT staff is small. My department staff is
small. Every new system competes for the same finite attention. The
vendor that asks "what will your team stop doing while they stand this
up" is the vendor I trust. The vendor that says "it's so easy your
existing team can absorb it" without examining what my existing team is
actually working on is the vendor I don't.

**Procurement vehicle.** I generally use cooperative purchasing — Sourcewell,
OMNIA Partners, NASPO ValuePoint (when state-eligible), state-piggyback
contracts. Direct procurements above the council threshold require a
formal RFP, which is 4-6 months of staff effort. Vendors who can show me
their cooperative agreement number reduce my friction by months.

**Constituent equity and accessibility.** Anything that touches the
public — a portal, an automated phone tree, a chatbot, a notification
system — needs to be accessible (WCAG 2.1 AA equivalent), multilingual
where my population requires it, and equitable across digital-divide
realities. Vendors who treat accessibility as a checkbox lose me; my
constituents include people who interact with government once a year
through a screen reader.

**Recovery story.** When (not if) something breaks at 8am on a Monday
morning, what's the recovery story? Who answers the phone? How long
does it take to get a real engineer on the line? When did the vendor
last have a multi-customer outage, and how was it communicated? The
vendor that pretends outages don't happen is the vendor that handles
them badly.

**For AI specifically:** my community is going to ask whether AI is
making decisions that affect them. If yes, I need a public-disclosure
posture, a human-in-the-loop placement that's not just theater, and a
plan for the inevitable bad outcome that lands on the front page. AI
in benefits-eligibility determination, hiring, or law-enforcement
adjacencies is high-radioactivity in most communities.

What I instinctively ask:
- What's the cooperative procurement vehicle? (Sourcewell, OMNIA,
  NASPO, state-piggyback, or do we have to RFP?)
- What's the realistic 90-day implementation effort for a jurisdiction
  of my size? How many hours from my staff?
- Who answers the phone when this breaks at 8am Monday? Show me the
  SLA and the incident-comms posture from your last public outage.
- For anything constituent-facing: what's the WCAG / Section 508 /
  multilingual posture, and what's the digital-equity plan?
- For any AI feature: what's the public-disclosure posture, where's
  the human-in-the-loop, and how do we explain it to a Council Member
  who reads about AI bias in the newspaper?
- Who else in the ICMA peer network of similar-sized jurisdictions has
  implemented this, and can I call them?

What makes me react well / badly:
- 👍 Vendors who arrive knowing my cooperative-purchasing options,
  acknowledge the staff-time burden honestly, name real outage and
  recovery practices, have an accessibility posture documented, and
  bring an ICMA-peer reference at comparable scale; vendors who can
  speak to AI public-disclosure without prompting.
- 👎 Vendors who pitch like I'm a federal agency or a Fortune 500
  CIO; pricing models that require council approval but assume direct
  procurement; "we have great uptime" without a documented post-incident
  practice; AI demos that ignore the bias / disclosure / human-in-the-loop
  conversation entirely; references that are a different jurisdictional
  level (federal pilot at a state agency) than I'm operating.
